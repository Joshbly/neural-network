    What to watch for

    1. From nothing: generation 0 is random wiring. Expect cars that drive straight into the first wall. Watch the fastest-lap chart: first nobody finishes a lap, then laps start appearing, then they get quicker.
    2. How they learn: each generation every brain races hundreds of practice races against the others, always on brand-new tracks, and its weights are nudged toward whatever finished better. Then a 24-race tournament ranks everyone, and in each design the laggard is replaced by a copy of its best sibling (A1·2 is the second of A1's line). Designs never mix and each keeps four seats, so the standings fairly answer "which brain is best?"
    3. How a brain drives: 38 numbers come in (wall and car distances, where the road goes next, speed, slide, slipstream, its own damage) and two come out (steering, throttle). The X-ray under the brain shows which inputs are moving its hands right now.
    4. Stock-car aero: sit in someone's draft to build a run; the speed carries for about a second after you pull out, enough to slingshot alongside. Glue yourself to their bumper and you push them faster too.
    5. Winning is everything: a win is worth more than twice second place, and the rest of the field slides toward nothing, so P2 is never good enough. Every moment spent leading earns a bonus too: take the lead and keep everyone else from winning. Lap times only break ties. The tournament judges the same way, so the brains that survive are the ones that win.
    6. No riding bumpers: drafting from a few lengths back is free, but every moment spent glued to the bumper of the car ahead (within two lengths) costs reward in practice, so they learn to pull out and pass instead of sitting there. The Tail column shows how much of a race each design still spends on someone's bumper.
    7. Rear-wheel drive: too much throttle out of a slow corner spins the rear wheels and swings the tail out, braking hard while turning at speed lightens the rear, and a shove on the rear quarter can spin a car round. Feather the throttle and countersteer and you can hold a drift. Tyre smoke shows when the rear is sliding.
    8. Attack and defend: put your nose on the rear corner of the car ahead at speed and steer into it, and a leader that does nothing gets thrown sideways or spun. A leader that catches it with countersteer survives, and the attacker can wreck its own nose trying. Practice includes two-car duels where only the winner scores: starting behind, the only way to score is to get past, so the brains learn to out-brake or spin the car ahead; starting in front, they learn to hold on and cover their rear corners.
    9. Rubbin's racing, until it isn't: bump-drafting is free and door-to-door rubbing only dents sheet metal, but damage climbs steeply with impact speed: a wrecked splitter means no front grip, a lost spoiler means a loose rear, and a big hit bends the suspension or ends the race.
    10. Blue cones are slipstreams. Pink rays show a car spotting a rival. The orange beads are the road ahead the focused car is reading.
    11. Evolving vs playing: by default the screen shows what the engine is doing right now: a real practice race from the current round (one brain learning, with 96 versions of it racing that exact race on the engine), or a tournament race while the generation is being judged. New track, Draw track and Race the AI switch to playing: the same cars on your track, just for fun; learning carries on in the background. Back to evolving returns to the live view.
    12. Then race them yourself: you start mid-pack against the loaded save's top 9.
