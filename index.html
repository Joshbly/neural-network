    What to watch for

    1. From nothing: generation 0 is random wiring. Expect cars that drive straight into the first wall. Watch the fastest-lap chart: first nobody finishes a lap, then laps start appearing, then they get quicker.
    2. How they learn: each generation every brain races hundreds of practice races against the others, always on brand-new tracks, and its weights are nudged toward whatever finished better. Then a 24-race tournament ranks everyone, and in each design a brain that's clearly behind over its last three tournaments is replaced by a slightly mutated copy of a strong sibling (A1·2 is the second of A1's family). No family may hold more than half a design's seats, so every design keeps at least two different ways of driving to evolve from. Designs never mix and each keeps four seats, so the standings fairly answer "which brain is best?"
    3. The rating: practice scores can't show progress, because everyone improves together. So every 5 generations each design's best races 96 races against frozen past champions on fixed tracks, and all the results ever recorded are turned into one rating per brain, like chess Elo: 400 points means 10 times stronger, generation 0 is 1000, and the shading shows how sure the number is. A rising line is real improvement.
    4. How a brain drives: 44 numbers come in (wall and car distances, how many seconds until it hits the car ahead, where the road goes next, how far it is from the edge of the racing surface and of the pavement (negative once it's down on the apron or the grass), how steep the banking is under it and in the corner ahead and whether the top lane is steeper, speed, slide, slipstream, its own damage) and two come out (steering, throttle). The X-ray under the brain shows which inputs are moving its hands right now.
    5. Neuron lab: click any neuron in the brain to see what it reacts to and what it does, then force it on, off or silence it and watch the car change. Every neuron works twice per decision, once on the real view and once on a mirror image, so a neuron that reacts to "a car on my right" also covers the left; forcing one acts on the real view only. The facts come from node train/neurons.js, which records the brain in 48 full races, measures every neuron, and races it with its most important neurons silenced.
    6. Stock-car aero: sit in someone's draft to build a run; the speed carries for about a second after you pull out, enough to slingshot alongside. Glue yourself to their bumper and you push them faster too: a pair nose to tail laps a plate track about 6% quicker than a car alone, but the pusher is only towing the leader to the line, and to win it has to pull out and lose the draft. Pull your nose up to someone's rear quarter and you side-draft them: their car slows and goes loose, because you've taken the air off their spoiler (a push through a corner does the same). Running two wide is slow for both, since neither car has clean air, so a long side-by-side fight lets the pack behind catch up.
    7. Winning is everything: a win is worth more than twice second place, and the rest of the field slides toward nothing, so P2 is never good enough. Leading most of the race and losing at the line is still losing: time in front and lap times only break ties. The tournament judges the same way, so the brains that survive are the ones that win.
    8. Riding bumpers: nothing in the scoring punishes it. Glued to someone's bumper you just push them toward the win, so the only way to score is to pull out and pass. The Tail column shows how much of a race each design still spends on someone's bumper, and Rub how much door to door.
    9. Rear-wheel drive: too much throttle out of a slow corner spins the rear wheels and swings the tail out, braking hard while turning at speed lightens the rear, and a shove on the rear quarter can spin a car round. Feather the throttle and countersteer and you can hold a drift. Tyre smoke shows when the rear is sliding.
    10. Attack and defend: put your nose on the rear corner of the car ahead at speed and steer into it, and a leader that does nothing gets thrown sideways or spun. A leader that catches it with countersteer survives, and the attacker can wreck its own nose trying. Practice includes two-car duels where only the winner scores: starting behind, the only way to score is to get past, so the brains learn to out-brake or spin the car ahead; starting in front, they learn to hold on and cover their rear corners.
    11. Rubbin's racing, until it isn't: bump-drafting is free and door-to-door rubbing only dents sheet metal, but damage climbs steeply with impact speed: a wrecked splitter means no front grip, a lost spoiler means a loose rear, and a big hit bends the suspension or ends the race. In stock cars a slam kills: hit the wall or another car at more than about 20 mph straight into it and the car may be wrecked on the spot, more likely the harder the hit, and the same crash sometimes limps on and sometimes doesn't. Leaning on the wall grinds the nose away, about a tenth of the downforce every 3 seconds, and can cut a tyre: a slow puncture that costs rear grip for a lap and a half. Two cars stuck door to door for more than a full second both lose a point of downforce every second until they part. Damage is more than lost downforce: a crumpled nose pushes and overheats the engine, a torn tail goes loose, wrecked bodywork drags, and a damaged car gets less out of the draft, suffers more in dirty air and can't push well. With 40% of the downforce gone a car is about 8% slower at the intermediates, 12% at the superspeedways, and with half of it gone race control parks it.
    12. Blue cones are slipstreams. Pink wedges are the directions the focused car feels rivals in, reaching to them and brighter the closer they are (a car between two directions lights both; arcade cars still see along thin rays). A dashed line to the car ahead, yellow turning red, counts down the seconds to a hit. Green-to-red rays find the walls, and the orange beads are the road ahead it's reading.
    13. Channels: every race on screen is one the engine really ran, car for car: the same track, grid, rules and weights, and each practice copy rebuilt from its own seed. Live follows the engine (practice, then the tournament); Practice and Duels show this round's practice; Tournament the latest tournament's official races and Rating the latest rating round, each checked against the official result at the flag. New track, Draw track and Race the AI switch to playing: the same cars on your track, just for fun; learning carries on in the background. Back to evolving returns to the channels.
    14. Then race them yourself: you start mid-pack against the loaded save's top 9.
    15. NASCAR mode: the 32 real ovals, from Daytona to Bowman Gray, traced from OpenStreetMap with their real banking, width, aprons and grass. The cars are real Cup car size (4.97 m × 1.99 m) and lap at real qualifying speed: about 190 mph at Daytona, 130 at Bristol, 95 at Martinsville. The five designs race as five teams, every car with its own number, colours and (made-up) sponsor.
    16. Packs and plates: at Daytona, Talladega and Atlanta the engines are choked to about 510 hp, so nobody can pull away alone: the draft is everything, cars run two and three wide in a pack, and a pushing line can sweep past. Everywhere else it's 670 hp, where handling in the corners matters more than the draft.
    17. Banking: the steep corners hold a car up like a wall of a bowl, so the high line at a progressively banked track like Bristol carries more speed. Run off the bottom onto the flat apron or the grass and the grip is gone; the infield grass and sand slow you hard.
    18. The yellow line: at the plate tracks, passing with all four wheels below the double yellow line is a black flag, and practice docks reward for any time spent down there.
    19. Flags: green to go; yellow for a spin, a stopped car or a wreck (the field freezes, follows the pace car, the first lapped car gets its lap back, and the car that caused it restarts at the back of its group); caution laps don't count, so a race always gets its full green distance; short races (practice, duels, under 5 laps) have no cautions at all, a spin only costs the car that spun; and a save's races stay green until it reaches generation 100 (young brains wreck too often for cautions to mean anything), with stage breaks still awarding points; double-file restarts in the restart zone; white for the last lap; checkered; overtime: a caution on the final lap means a green-white-checkered, two attempts, then the next caution ends it under yellow; red for a big wreck; black for a stop-and-go; red-and-black parks a car too damaged to go on; blue-and-yellow tells lapped cars the leaders are coming. Tournament races have two stage breaks. No pit stops, fuel or tyre wear: races are sprints, and damage is for keeps.
